Rol: Product Designer (process mapping, UX, cross-functional alignment)

Context and problem

A large-scale retail company ran a promotional process that involved multiple teams operating at different moments with different tools. The system was built in stages — not designed as a whole.


This created:

  • Duplicated work and loss of context between teams.
  • Dependencies between areas that were never clearly defined.
  • Critical knowledge that lived in people, not in visible artifacts.
  • Friction that slowed down promotion launches and generated constant rework.

My approach / strategy

The focus wasn't on individual users — it was on the coordination between them. I led the end-to-end process mapping to build a shared vision that didn't exist before.

  • Defined the research plan and prioritized interviews with key stakeholders by area.
  • Mapped the current state (as-is) — tools, approvals, dependencies and friction points.
  • Discovered that information was being re-entered between teams, that decisions depended on unrecorded previous conversations, and that each area had a different interpretation of the same process step — that was the root of the rework.
  • With that vision, proposed a shared model (to-be) that aligned timelines, roles and responsibilities I worked in an agile way with Product and Engineering to ensure a viable and fast implementation..

Solution and key decisions

  • Systemic vision: built the first complete map of the process — visible to all areas involved for the first time.
  • Shared model: unified the way each team understood and operated the promotional process, eliminating different interpretations of the same flow.
  • Selective automation: identified the highest-impact points and introduced automation where it added the most value — without trying to automate everything
  • Future blueprint: translated the new model into wireframes and prototypes the team could use as a clear reference, and designed the ideal future state as a foundation for the next iterations.

Impact / results

Operational alignment and visibility across teams

I identified that several existing flows had too many actions that distracted users instead of helping them. I defended and justified the need to simplify — removing unnecessary steps and focusing each flow on one clear task. The result was a more direct experience, with less noise and easier decisions to make.

  • Built a complete map of the process that, for the first time, allowed all areas involved to visualize how their work connected to the rest.
  • Simplified flows by eliminating actions that distracted users.
  • Design decisions defended and justified to product and engineering.
  • Teams aligned around a common operating model — documented and visible for the first time.
  • A solid foundation to scale improvements without depending on individual knowledge.

Rol: Product Designer — UX, UI, research-to-product translation, flow and business rules definition, alignment with business and engineering.

Context and problem

A large-scale retail company used a tablet app for inspectors to record and approve perishable merchandise. Operations took place across different contexts — from refrigerated chambers to distribution centers and field work.


The existing tool had been built as a generic solution for different products and scenarios, but it didn't adapt well to real operations.

This created several friction points:

  • Overly generic data capture: the app displayed fields that didn't apply to all products, causing confusion and unnecessary work.
  • Rigid business rules: adding or modifying specifications required development intervention.
  • Difficult decisions in the field: inspectors needed to consult supervisors or rely on samples to determine whether merchandise should be approved.
  • Connectivity issues: in scenarios without a stable connection, information could be lost or not recorded correctly.

Working in refrigerated chambers required special clothing and gloves to protect against extreme cold, which made it difficult to handle the tablet. The battery drained quickly due to the temperature and, without auto-save, losing collected data was a real risk. Pen and paper was more reliable.



My approach / strategy

I joined the project after the research phase. My role was to analyze existing journeys, pain points and real field usage data, and translate those findings into product and design decisions.
From that information I identified that the problem wasn't only capture speed. The product also wasn't designed to adapt to the operation it was meant to support.

I defined the strategy around two connected needs:

  • Inspectors: a fast, clear experience focused only on the information relevant to the product they were inspecting.
  • Administrators: a flexible way to configure specifications, rules and exceptions without depending on development.

This led to designing not just a new inspection experience, but also the system that allowed it to be configured.


Solution and key decisions

Role-differentiated experiences


I designed specific experiences for inspectors and administrators. The inspector saw only the information and actions needed to complete an inspection, while the administrator had tools to manage the rules that shaped that experience.

Managed within the product, eliminating constant development requests
I designed a desktop business rules system that allowed administrators to create and modify specifications based on the product and operating conditions. This included scenarios such as out-of-season products or exceptions to acceptance criteria, defined by the business teams. The goal was for rule changes to be managed from within the product, rather than becoming constant requests to development.

Product-specific data capture
Instead of presenting a generic list of fields for all products, I structured the experience so inspectors focused only on the characteristics relevant to the product they were evaluating. This reduced unnecessary information and made decision-making during inspections easier.

Visual aids for decision-making
I incorporated visual references within the experience to help inspectors determine whether a product met acceptance criteria. The intent was to bring the knowledge needed to make that decision directly to the point of inspection, reducing the need to constantly consult other people.

Design for offline scenarios
Operations didn't always occur with a stable connection. To prevent data loss during an inspection, I incorporated auto-save and accounted for how the experience would behave when connectivity was unavailable.

Impact / results

The new solution was approved by the client and adopted for operations.

  • Less friction for inspectors: the experience adapted to the product being inspected, eliminating information they didn't need.
  • Greater autonomy for the business: administrators could configure new products, rules and exceptions without depending directly on development.
  • Clearer decisions in the field: relevant references and criteria were available within the inspection experience.
  • More reliable data capture: auto-save reduced the risk of losing information in low-connectivity scenarios.
  • Sustained adoption: Four years after launch, the tool remains in active operational use.

More than redesigning an existing app, the project turned a generic and underused tool into a system adaptable to the real needs of the operation.

Rol: Product Designer (discovery, UX, UI, design system, alignment with development)

Context and problem

A company operated without a centralized system to record product entries, exits and supply movements. At the end of each year, they relied on a manual inventory count to justify expenses and request the following year's budget.


This created several problems:

  • No reliable record of product inventory.
  • Expenses difficult to justify due to lack of traceability.
  • Poor communication between areas when requesting materials and sharing documents.

The business needed a tool that centralized control and provided real visibility into inventory and movements.


My approach / strategy

I led the design of a digital product from scratch, starting from a context with no prior data and no formalized processes.

  • Discovery with users and stakeholders to understand needs, flows and friction points.
  • Definition of a research plan with clear objectives and concrete steps.
  • Interviews to understand how they worked, how they interacted with other areas, and how they shared documents when requesting materials.

With that foundation, the goal was to centralize control of entries, exits and supply, create a clear and easy-to-manage structure for users, and provide a solid product direction for development to build from.

Solution and key decisions

The challenge wasn't just designing screens — it was building a product from scratch in a context with no prior data, no formalized processes, and no reference for how it should work. Every decision had to be justified from the problem itself.

  • Discovery without prior data: designed a questionnaire from scratch to gather initial information and conducted interviews to understand flows, roles and friction points between areas.
  • Visibility-centered architecture: defined the key sections of the product — dashboard, reports, items, orders and warehouse — prioritizing that any user could understand the inventory status at a glance.
  • Design system from scratch: developed typography, color palette, button states, messages and iconography in collaboration with development to ensure consistency and fast implementation.
  • High-fidelity screens: delivered a clear interface for recording and managing entries, exits and supply movements, ready for development.



Impact / results

From manual process to real-time digital visibility


The product transformed a manual, decentralized process into a structured digital tool. For the first time, the team had real visibility into inventory and traceability of movements — eliminating dependence on the manual count at year end.

  • Real inventory visibility and movement traceability for the first time.
  • Eliminated dependence on the manual count at year end.
  • Design system delivered as a solid foundation for development to scale the solution.
  • A clear architecture that allowed the team to manage entries, exits and supply from a single place.
